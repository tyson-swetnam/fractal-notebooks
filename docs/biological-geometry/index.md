**Architectures of the Infinite: Self-Affine Fractals, Complex Dimensions, and the Thermodynamic Geometries of Biological Space-Filling**

Organisms must capture diffuse resources while minimising the metabolic cost of the tissue that
reaches them, and this chapter argues that evolution solves the problem with self-affine rather
than self-similar architectures. It extends the West-Brown-Enquist model, whose radial and
longitudinal branching ratios \( \beta_r = n^{-1/2} \) and \( \beta_l = n^{-1/3} \) differ, to
the complex dimensions that arise from finite-scale fractality. Three space-filling mechanisms
are examined: the four affine maps of the Barnsley fern as a compressed growth algorithm,
diffusion-limited aggregation in lichens, algae and kelp with fractal dimensions near 1.6 to 1.7,
and competitive canopy packing through crown shyness, weighted Voronoi tessellations and
Apollonian circle packing. Log-periodic oscillations in growth data are read as signatures of
discrete scale invariance, leading to the proposal that organisms act as zeta-function machines
whose stable forms are eigenstates of their own spectral geometry.
